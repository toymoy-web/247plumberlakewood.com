From Dairy Valley To Little Hills

Cerritos Grew Fast, And Grew Later Than Lakewood

Cerritos borders Lakewood directly to the northeast, about 6 miles away, and like several of its neighbors it has an agricultural origin story. The city was incorporated in 1956 under the name Dairy Valley, a reaction to nearby Artesia's rapid urbanization at the time. It operated as a dairy farming community before land values and rising property taxes pushed most dairies out to Chino and the Central Valley through the early 1960s.

The name changed to Cerritos, Spanish for "little hills," in 1967, right as the city entered an extraordinary growth period. Between 1970 and 1972, Cerritos was the fastest-growing city in California. Its population jumped from roughly 16,000 to 38,000 in just two years as dairy pastures gave way to planned residential subdivisions.

That rapid, concentrated build-out is actually a point in Cerritos's favor for plumbing purposes: unlike Bellflower or Paramount, where construction stretched across several decades, most of Cerritos's housing dates to this same tight early-1970s window. It behaves more like a second version of Lakewood's own single-era construction pattern, just two decades later and with copper and early PEX more common than the galvanized lines typical of Lakewood's 1950s homes.

Cerritos is also known regionally for the Cerritos Auto Square, one of the largest auto malls in the country, the Cerritos Center for the Performing Arts, and an award-winning public library, all developed using sales-tax revenue from the Los Cerritos Center mall built in 1971. None of that affects residential plumbing directly, but it's part of why the city looks and feels different from its more gradually-developed neighbors.
