A Small, Dense City

Bell's Housing Stock Is Older Than Most Neighbors

Bell is one of the smaller cities we cover, at just 2.6 square miles, but it's also one of the most densely built, packing a large population into that small footprint. The city incorporated back in 1927, and most of its housing is made up of bungalows built between the 1920s and 1940s, decades before Lakewood's postwar tract homes went up.

That older construction window means different plumbing problems than we see elsewhere in our service area. Original galvanized supply lines from the 1920s and 30s are now a full century old in some homes, well past what even Lakewood's 1950s pipe is dealing with, and small lot sizes mean shared or tightly-run drain lines between neighboring properties are more common. Our drain cleaning team sees this pattern often in Bell.

Bell's population density, among the highest per square mile of any city in our service area, also means more multi-unit conversions of what were originally single-family bungalows. A property that started as one household's plumbing sometimes now serves two or three units, and that kind of retrofit is worth a specific look rather than assuming the original 1920s-era plumbing was sized for today's usage.

The James George Bell House, the city's namesake landmark on East Gage Avenue, dates back to the 1870s and stands as a reminder of just how much older this area is than most of the postwar suburbs nearby. For a homeowner, the practical version of that history is simple: assume original plumbing here is older than average, and budget accordingly for a repair versus a replacement decision.
