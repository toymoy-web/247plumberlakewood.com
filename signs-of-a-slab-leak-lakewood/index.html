Signs of a Slab Leak in Your Lakewood, CA Home

By the Lakewood 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-05

Talk To A Plumber
(562) 488-9614
Call Now
Homeowner Guide

Slab leaks come up often enough in Lakewood that most homeowners here eventually hear the term, whether or not they've dealt with one. Most of the city's homes were built directly on a concrete slab foundation during the same 1950 to 1953 construction window, with copper supply lines running underneath. A leak in one of those lines is a real and fairly common failure point once a house reaches this age.

The tricky part is that a slab leak doesn't always announce itself clearly. Here's what actually points to one, and what doesn't.

The Signs Worth Taking Seriously

A Warm Spot On The Floor

A hot water line leaking under the slab often warms the flooring directly above it, most noticeable through tile or thin flooring near a garage or bathroom.

An Unexplained Water Bill Increase

If usage habits haven't changed but the bill jumped, a running leak somewhere in the system is the most common explanation.

The Sound Of Running Water

Hearing water move with every fixture in the house shut off points to a leak somewhere in the line between the meter and the house.

Cracks In Flooring Or Baseboards

Water pressure building under a slab over time can shift flooring and create small, unexplained cracks that weren't there before.

What Doesn't Necessarily Mean A Slab Leak

Not every damp spot or water bill increase means the problem is under the slab. A running toilet is a far more common and much cheaper cause of a water bill spike, and it's worth ruling out first since it takes minutes to check. Condensation around a water heater or under a sink is also frequently mistaken for a leak when it's actually just normal humidity buildup in a poorly ventilated space.

Musty odors alone, without any of the signs above, are also not a reliable indicator on their own. Southern California's climate and older construction can produce a musty smell in a garage or crawlspace for reasons that have nothing to do with a slab leak specifically.

Why Guessing Costs More Than Checking

The instinct when you suspect a slab leak is understandable: open the floor where the warm spot is and look. In practice, this often means opening the wrong section, since a leak under a slab doesn't always show its heat signature directly above the actual break point. Electronic leak detection equipment locates the leak's real position before any concrete is touched, which is the difference between one targeted repair and repeated demolition in the wrong spots.

If It Turns Out To Be A Slab Leak

Once a leak is confirmed and located, slab leak repair typically comes down to a choice between a spot repair, opening just the section with the leak, or rerouting the line above the slab to avoid future concrete work entirely. Which one makes sense depends on the line's history: a first-time leak in an otherwise sound system usually favors a spot repair, while a line that's already leaked once before is often a better candidate for rerouting.

A Word On Timing

A slab leak rarely gets better on its own. The longer a hot water line leaks under a foundation, the more water is wasted and the more opportunity there is for the leak to affect flooring, baseboards, or in less common cases, the foundation itself. None of this means panic is warranted, a slab leak is a manageable, well-understood repair, but it does mean it's genuinely worth addressing once confirmed rather than monitoring indefinitely for months on end.
