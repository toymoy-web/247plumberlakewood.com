From Dairy Farms To Little India

Artesia's Identity Changed, Its Housing Age Mostly Didn't

Artesia borders Lakewood to the east, a small city of just over a square mile that's best known today as the heart of Southern California's "Little India," the stretch of Pioneer Boulevard lined with South Asian restaurants, sari shops, and jewelers. That identity is relatively recent, developing from the early 1980s onward. For the eight decades before that, Artesia was something else entirely: one of the region's major dairy farming districts.

The village was first settled in 1875, named for the artesian wells that made the land valuable for farming, and Dutch and Portuguese immigrant families built it into a significant dairy center through the 1920s, 30s, and beyond. Artesia didn't incorporate as its own city until 1959, two years after Lakewood, by which point postwar development pressure was already pushing most dairy operations out to Chino and the Central Valley.

Some of Artesia's older, larger homes are thought to be remnants of that farming-dynasty era, built by families who'd done well in dairy before the land converted to residential and commercial use. The rest of the city's housing dates to the same general postwar period as Lakewood's, though spread across a longer stretch of years rather than concentrated in one three-year window.

In practical terms, that means an Artesia home's plumbing age tracks more closely with Lakewood's than some of our other service areas, similar postwar materials, though without Lakewood's unusual single-era uniformity.
