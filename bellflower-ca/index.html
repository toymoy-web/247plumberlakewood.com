From Dairy Farms To Dense Suburb

Bellflower's Construction Spans Decades, Not One Boom

Bellflower is Lakewood's closest neighbor of any kind, directly bordering the city to the north along roughly a three-mile stretch. The city was founded in 1906 and incorporated in 1957, and for decades before that it was known locally as the "Cream City," home to as many as 250 separate dairy farms run largely by Dutch, Japanese, and Portuguese families who settled the area.

That agricultural history matters for plumbing the same way it does in Paramount and Cerritos just to the east: Bellflower's housing didn't go up in one uniform postwar burst the way Lakewood's did. Development continued gradually through the 1950s and 60s as dairy land converted to residential tracts, so pipe age and material vary more block to block than in a single-era city.

Bellflower was briefly going to be called "Somerset" when the post office was established in 1909, but the name was rejected to avoid confusion with Somerset, Colorado. The city ultimately took its name from the yellow bellflower apple grown in local orchards, a detail still visible in the city's branding and its "Growing Together" motto.

In practical terms, a Bellflower home's plumbing age is less predictable from the street than a Lakewood home's. We check the actual pipe material and water heater age on the first visit rather than assuming a single citywide construction era.
