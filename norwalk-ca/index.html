Sugar Beets, Not Dairy

Norwalk Grew Differently Than Its Neighbors

Norwalk sits east of Lakewood and is a different kind of neighbor than most of our other service areas. With over 100,000 residents across nearly 10 square miles, it's one of the larger cities we cover, roughly the same population as Lakewood but spread across a noticeably bigger footprint. It was incorporated in 1957, three years after Lakewood, and its name has an unusual origin for this part of Southern California.

Settlers Atwood and Gilbert Sproul originally named the area "Corvallis" after their former home in Oregon when they settled here in the 1870s. The name was changed to Norwalk in 1879, after the Connecticut hometown of another early settler, and the name stuck even though nothing else about the area has a New England connection.

Norwalk's economy was built on sugar beet farming rather than the dairy industry that shaped so many of its neighbors, among the largest sugar beet operations in Southern California during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Much of the land had originally been part of Rancho Los Nietos and Rancho Los Cerritos, Spanish and Mexican-era land grants that once covered a huge swath of this part of the county.

Given Norwalk's size, its housing stock spans a wider range of construction eras than a compact city like Hawaiian Gardens or Artesia, from early postwar tracts through later 20th-century development. We confirm the specific neighborhood and approximate build era on the first visit rather than assuming a single citywide pattern the way we might for Lakewood itself.
