Galvanized vs. Copper Pipes in Lakewood, CA Homes

By the Lakewood 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026-08-15

Homeowner Guide

If you own a home in Lakewood, there's a good chance you've wondered exactly what kind of pipe is running behind your walls and under your slab. Since most of the city was built in the same three-year window between 1950 and 1953, a large share of homes here started out with the same original plumbing. Knowing which material yours has left tells you a lot about what to expect going forward.

You don't need to open a wall to get a reasonable idea. A few visible clues, checked in the right places, usually tell the story.

Where To Look First

The easiest place to check is under a sink, in a garage, or near the water heater, anywhere a section of supply line is exposed rather than buried in a wall or slab. Look at the pipe itself, not the fixture it connects to.

Galvanized Steel

Dull gray color, often with visible threaded joints, and it's magnetic, a magnet will stick to it. Rust-colored staining near joints is common on older sections.

Copper

Reddish-orange when new, sometimes weathered to a dull brown or greenish patina over time. Joints are typically soldered, a smooth solder bead, rather than threaded. Not magnetic.

A third material, PEX, is a flexible colored plastic (usually red, blue, or white) that became common in later repipes and remodels. If you see this, it usually means at least part of the home's plumbing has already been updated from the original material.

What The Material Actually Means For You

Original galvanized pipe from Lakewood's 1950-53 construction window is now more than seventy years old, well past the material's typical service life. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, which means the interior can be significantly narrowed by rust and mineral buildup even when the outside looks intact. This is a common cause of gradually declining water pressure that a homeowner sometimes mistakes for a fixture problem.

Copper generally holds up longer, but it isn't immune to failure. Pinhole leaks in copper lines, small perforations that develop from water chemistry or reaction with certain soil conditions, are one of the more common reasons for a hidden leak in a copper-plumbed home. If you're seeing an unexplained water bill increase or a damp spot with no obvious source, that's worth a leak detection visit regardless of which material you have, since the diagnostic approach differs by material and location.

Water Heater Connections Are A Quick Tell

The flexible connector lines running into and out of a water heater are usually easy to see without any tools, and they're a fast way to spot corrosion even if you can't easily check the main supply lines elsewhere in the house. If those connectors show heavy rust or white mineral crusting, it's a reasonable signal that the water heater itself may be due for service. Our water heater repair team checks this as a standard part of any service call.

When It's Worth A Professional Look

Visual identification is a useful first step, but it has limits. It won't tell you the condition of pipe sections buried in a slab or running through a wall, and corrosion inside a pipe usually isn't visible from the outside at all. If you're dealing with declining water pressure or discolored water, or you're simply buying a home this age and want to know what you're working with, a technician can check accessible sections and advise on next steps. That's a better approach than assuming the worst from a single data point.

For drain lines specifically, the visible-pipe-material trick doesn't apply the same way, since most homes this age ran cast iron for drains regardless of what supply line material they used. If a drain has been slow or clogging repeatedly, that's a separate question better answered by drain cleaning and, where warranted, a camera inspection of the line itself.

Signs It's Worth Calling A Plumber Rather Than Waiting

  • Visible rust-colored water, especially right after the water has sat unused for several hours
  • A noticeable, gradual drop in water pressure with no other obvious cause
  • Any wet spot, staining, or bubbling near an exposed pipe joint
  • Green or blue-green staining near a copper joint, which can indicate a slow leak reacting with the metal
  • A pipe section that feels unusually corroded, flaky, or thin to the touch
